The most painful and least answered question in the communist tradition
is also the simplest. Once a workers' party survives long enough to
have a payroll, an apparatus, and a generation of leaders whose income
depends on the institution they helped to build, what stops it from
turning into the opposite of what it was for? Hal Draper, writing in
1971, called the resulting formation a *micro-sect*: a body that "is a
miniaturized version of the revolutionary party-to-be... a microscopic
edition or model of the mass party that does not yet exist," held
together by "iron hoops such as are needed to hold together the staves
of a crumbling barrel."[^draper-anatomy] That image (iron hoops around
a crumbling barrel) is what we mean by **ossification**. And bonesaw is
what cuts it.

This is the editorial line of *Bonesaw*, stated openly so it can be
disputed openly.

## The diagnosis is older than Stalinism

The temptation is to date the bureaucratic deformation of the workers'
movement to a single event. The ban on factions in 1921. The rise of
Stalin. The defeat of the Left Opposition. We think this misreads the
record. The diagnosis precedes any of those moments. In 1904, reviewing
*What Is To Be Done?*, Rosa Luxemburg already described the Bolshevik
organisational schema as one in which "the central committee appears
as the only active element of the party, and all the other
organisations simply as the tools which implement its decisions."[^luxemburg]
She did not deny that centralism was a real tendency in social
democracy. She insisted instead that "Social Democratic centralism has
most of all a coordinating, synthetic character and not a regulative
and exclusive one,"[^luxemburg-spirit] and warned that no statute
could substitute for the political life that gave statutes their
meaning.

Trotsky, in the same year, sharpened the warning into the formulation
that has been quoted ever since:

> The organisation of the Party substitutes itself for the party as a
> whole; then the Central Committee substitutes itself for the
> organisation; and finally the "dictator" substitutes himself for the
> Central Committee.[^trotsky-1904]

Trotsky later joined the party he had so accurately described, and
became one of its central figures. The substitution he warned of did
in fact occur, and Trotsky himself was among its first political
victims. None of this collapses the warning. What it does is establish
that the danger was identified by serious revolutionaries before the
revolution itself, before the Civil War, before the famine, before
NEP, and before the rise of any one named individual.

## The bureaucracy is not an external graft

A common consolation among those still committed to the tradition is
that the bureaucratic deformation of Soviet power was the result of
external pressure: civil war, encirclement, the failure of the German
revolution, the destruction of the most advanced section of the
Petrograd working class. All of this is historically true.[^smith-civil-war]
But it is not a defence of the *organisational logic* that
collaborated with those pressures rather than resisting them. By 1920,
the Democratic Centralists, who were themselves Bolsheviks of long
standing, were already arguing that "a dictatorship of the party
bureaucracy was being established as elections were being replaced by
appointments and transfers."[^farber-dc] They were correct. By the
time Lenin in his last years was writing that "we are being sucked
into a foul, bureaucratic swamp,"[^smith-swamp] the disease was no
longer a deviation; it was the operating system.

What kept the Soviet party-state functioning, S. A. Smith argues, was
not bureaucracy in its proper Weberian sense at all, but rather
"bureaucracy galvanized by clientelism": local bosses presiding over
"personal fiefdoms" and relying on "family circles" of mutual
protection.[^smith-clientelism] This is what ossification looks like
in practice. It is not the rigid application of rules. It is the
opposite. It is the replacement of accountable rules by the protected
networks of those who can no longer afford accountability.

## Against the cure that reproduces the disease

The Democratic Centralists, to their credit, identified the problem
early. To their tragedy, their proposed remedy was, as Samuel Farber
puts it, an attempt to "combat bureaucracy by bureaucratic means,
maintaining that an improved mechanism would facilitate the practice
of a more democratic centralism."[^farber-dc-cure] This is the
characteristic move of the ossified party in mid-decay: the conviction
that what failed was the implementation, not the form.

The opposite error is also a real one. Chris Harman, in his 1968
defence of the Leninist conception against Luxemburg's critique,
charged Luxemburg with a "strong fatalism in her hope that the
'unconscious' will be able to correct the 'conscious'."[^harman]
There is something to this. Jo Freeman, writing about the women's
liberation movement in the United States, made a parallel argument
from a very different angle: an organisation that refuses formal
structure does not abolish power within itself, it merely makes that
power unaccountable, since "the informal groups' vested interests will
be sustained by the informal structures which exist, and the movement
will have no way of determining who shall exercise power within
it."[^freeman] Spontaneity, in other words, is not the cure for
bureaucracy. It is what bureaucracy preys on.

So we are left with two failure modes. The party that ossifies into
its own apparatus, and the movement that refuses structure and is
ossified instead by the unaccountable cliques that fill the vacuum.
Bonesaw is the wager that there is something worth saying between
these two failures, and that the saying of it requires looking
honestly at the historical record rather than at any of the
hagiographies built on top of it.

## What this blog will and will not do

We will publish work that takes the communist tradition seriously
enough to argue with. We will cite our sources, including sources we
disagree with, and we will quote them at length where the quotation
matters. We will treat the disputes between Luxemburg and Lenin,
between the Workers' Opposition and the Tenth Congress, between
Trotsky and Stalin, and between Draper and the American Trotskyists
who came before him, as live disputes rather than settled ones.

We will not pretend that the twentieth century is a closed file. We
will also not pretend it can be reopened by repeating the slogans of
1917 louder. Draper put the standing problem with admirable directness
and we cannot improve on his phrasing: "There is a road to a
revolutionary party which is not the road of the sect."[^draper-road]
What that road is, what its predecessors got wrong about it, and what
its current claimants are still getting wrong about it, is what this
blog is for.

The bonesaw is not a metaphor for purges. It is a metaphor for the
discipline of cutting away dead bone before it kills the patient.
